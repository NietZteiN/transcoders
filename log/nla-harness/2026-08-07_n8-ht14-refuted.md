### Target Date: 2026-08-07 (N8 / HT14 — **REFUTED**, and the significant log-rank is a confound, not a finding)

- **Hypotheses / what we're testing:** **HT14 (confirmatory)** — the model's eventual answer surfaces in the NLA reads *earlier* (in relative trace position) in correct runs than wrong ones. Measured with the NLA itself, per the user's explicit choice over a logit-lens. Rule frozen 2026-08-06: `mentions_answer` = literal ∨ numeric (tol 1e-9) ∨ container (≥⅔ in order); primary statistic = **excess over a 20-draw foreign-answer null** matched on tier/type/digit-count; onset = first of 10 `u` bins with own hit-rate ≥0.5 that *stays* ≥0.5; never reached ⇒ **right-censored, not dropped**; test = tier-stratified log-rank. **CONFIRM = log-rank p<.05 AND correct median onset ≥0.10 earlier.**

- **Setup:** `src/analysis/n8_answer_onset.py`, env `transcoders-mi`, **CPU only**. Seed 20260724, `N_BOOT=4000`, 10 bins, 20 foreign answers/read. Input = the N5 dense corpus (4,653 reads / 182 cases) joined to `captures.jsonl` for `model_answer` — **the model's own answer, not ground truth**, since HT14 is about answer *formation*, not correctness. Output `data/nla/n8/2026-08-07/ht14.json`.
  - **Group-structure check applied in advance** (the HT12 lesson): onset is one number per case, so `correct` is constant within case. The frozen primary is already a **case-level** survival test with no per-case random effect, so it is immune; it was deliberately *not* "upgraded" to a read-level mixed model. Flagged in the [pre-run addendum](2026-08-07_n7-prerun-power-addendum.md) before running.
  - `lifelines` is absent from the env, so Kaplan–Meier and the stratified log-rank are implemented directly (~40 lines) rather than adding a dependency mid-project. The matcher was unit-tested on 9 hand-built positives/negatives first (**9/9**, incl. word-boundary `927`≠`9271`, `3`≡`3.0`, container order-sensitivity, hex).

- **Results (primary arm — cheap answers excluded, 137 cases):**

  | quantity | correct | wrong |
  |---|---|---|
  | cases reaching onset | **15.5%** (9/58) | **2.5%** (2/79) |
  | median onset | **undefined** | **undefined** |
  | mean excess over foreign null | +0.0148 [−0.016, +0.047] | **−0.0211 [−0.032, −0.010]** |

  - **92% of cases never reach onset at all.** Both KM curves stay above 0.5, so the median is undefined and the "≥0.10 earlier" criterion is **unmeasurable in principle**, not merely unmet.
  - Tier-stratified **log-rank χ²=9.54, p=0.0020**, O−E=+4.62 — significant, in the predicted direction.
  - **Base rate: own-answer hit rate 2.8% vs foreign-answer null 3.6%.** Across the corpus the reads match the model's *own* answer **less often than a random same-tier answer**.
  - correct − wrong excess = +0.0359 [+0.0032, +0.0692].

- **What worked / hypothesis verdict:** **HT14 ✗ REFUTED.** The frozen CONFIRM needs both the log-rank *and* a ≥0.10-earlier median; under 92% censoring the median does not exist, so CONFIRM is unreachable. But the more important finding is that **the significant log-rank should not be reported as support**, for a reason I went looking for rather than waiting to be asked:

  **The onset statistic has no null protection, and the groups differ in answer *string properties*.** Wrong-case answers are dramatically rarer and longer than correct-case ones — median **0** other cases share the string vs **5**, and median **16** chars vs **7** (Mann-Whitney **p = 1.3×10⁻¹⁴**). A short, common answer (`7`, `[1, 2]`) is far more likely to appear by chance inside a ~650-character read than a long, unique one. The frozen design put the foreign-answer null on the **excess trajectory** but computed **onset from raw own-hit rates**, so the log-rank is fully exposed to this confound. The 15.5%-vs-2.5% onset gap is what answer-string commonness predicts on its own.

  **Exploratory check (labelled, no FDR):** re-matching the foreign null on answer length bucket as well leaves the excess gap essentially unchanged (+0.0341 vs +0.0359) but with the bootstrap CI now touching zero, **[0.0000, +0.0679]** — and leaves the onset/log-rank *completely* unchanged (0.025 vs 0.155, p identical), because no null enters that statistic. That is the diagnostic: the part of the result a null can touch is fragile; the part that looks strongest is precisely the part no null was applied to.

- **Observations:**
  1. **The honest headline is a measurement-floor result: NLA reads at L20 do not carry the model's eventual answer in any usable way.** Own-rate 2.8% *below* a 3.6% chance floor. HT14 is not so much refuted as unanswerable with this instrument at this layer — and the pre-registered foreign-answer null is what made that visible instead of letting a 2.8% hit rate read as a trajectory.
  2. **Design lesson, and it generalises:** a null must be attached to the *statistic that carries the verdict*, not only to the descriptive curve alongside it. HT14's null guarded the trajectory while the log-rank — the actual decision rule — ran unguarded. Any future onset/threshold statistic needs its own null.
  3. Pre-specifying the cheap-answer exclusion earned its keep. The `with_cheap_answers` arm shows a *stronger* apparent effect (excess +0.0411, CI excluding 0; log-rank p=2.9×10⁻⁵) — which is exactly backwards, since `0`/`1`/`2`/`true` are where spurious matching is worst. Had that been the primary it would have read as a clean confirmation.
  4. Two of three confirmatory hypotheses are now refuted **by mechanism, not by weak data**: HT12 was reply length, HT14 is answer-string commonness. In both cases the effect was real in the raw numbers and vanished (or was explained) under a control that was written down in advance.

- **New questions / new hypotheses:** (a) Is the answer simply *not linearly present* at L20, or is the NLA verbalizer the bottleneck? The reads are prose summaries of a state — an answer could be encoded and never verbalized. A logit-lens/probe comparison at the same positions would separate "not represented" from "not verbalized" — and this is now the more informative version of the question the user originally asked. (b) If a future onset analysis is run, match the null on **commonness and length**, and apply it to the onset statistic itself.
